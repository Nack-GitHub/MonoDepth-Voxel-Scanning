\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:related}
% Thai version: 02_related_work_th.tex (main_short_th.tex); keep the two in step.

This section explains the theory the study rests on---why one image does not fix depth in metres, how the model we
fine-tune is built and trained, and how depth maps become a room---and places the study among prior work on each point.

\subsection{Depth from a single image}
In the pinhole model of a camera~\cite{hartley}, the size of an object in the image is proportional to its real size
divided by its distance, multiplied by the focal length of the lens. A room twice as large seen from twice as far
therefore gives the same image, so geometry alone cannot recover depth in metres from one image; a network can only
infer it from learned cues, such as the usual height of a door or a table, and even these cues depend on the focal
length, because the same object at the same distance looks larger through a longer lens. Eigen et al.~\cite{eigen14}
trained an early deep network for this task and, because of this ambiguity, measured its error on the logarithm of
depth in a way that separates an error in the overall scale of the scene from an error in its shape. A weighted form
of this scale-invariant logarithmic loss, known as SiLog, also trains ZoeDepth~\cite{zoedepth} and the fine-tunes of
this study.

Two families of models handle the missing scale differently. Relative models give it up: MiDaS~\cite{midas} trained one
network on a mixture of datasets whose depths came from different sensors and units, which is possible only if the loss
ignores the scale and offset of each image. Its output is therefore inverse depth (disparity) that is correct only up
to an unknown scale and offset per image, and these two numbers must be found from other data before the output gives
metres. Depth Anything~\cite{da1} and Depth Anything~V2~\cite{da2} (\mbox{DA-v2}) keep this output but train on far more
images. Metric models output metres directly by learning the scale from labelled data. ZoeDepth~\cite{zoedepth} attaches
metric heads, fine-tuned on NYU Depth~v2~\cite{nyuv2} and KITTI~\cite{kitti}, to a relative model, and \mbox{DA-v2} releases
metric variants fine-tuned from its relative model, the indoor one (\mbox{DA-v2} Metric-Indoor) on the rendered rooms of
Hypersim~\cite{hypersim}. A further line addresses the focal length: Metric3D~\cite{metric3d} maps every image to a
canonical camera with a fixed focal length, UniDepth~\cite{unidepth} predicts the camera together with the depth, and
Depth Pro~\cite{depthpro} estimates the focal length when it is not given. These works report per-image metrics on
benchmarks such as NYU Depth~v2 and KITTI. Because such metrics average over independent images, they cannot show
whether a model's scale stays the same across the frames of one scan, which is what matters once the frames are fused
into one room.

\subsection{Architecture and training of Depth Anything V2}
\mbox{DA-v2} consists of an image encoder and a depth decoder. The encoder is DINOv2~\cite{dinov2}, a vision transformer
(ViT)~\cite{vit}: it cuts the image into small square patches and lets every patch draw information from every other
patch through a mechanism called self-attention. DINOv2 was pretrained without labels on a large curated collection of
images, so that its features serve many tasks. The decoder is the Dense Prediction Transformer (DPT)~\cite{dpt}, which
takes features from several encoder layers, reassembles them into image-like maps at several resolutions and fuses
these into one dense depth map. The released code splits the decoder into a neck, which reassembles and fuses, and a
head, which outputs depth; as in the Introduction, ``DPT head'' in this paper means both.

\mbox{DA-v2} is itself trained with a teacher. A large model is first trained on synthetic images only, whose depth is exact;
it then labels about 62 million real images that have no depth, and the released models learn from these model-made
labels, called pseudo-labels~\cite{da2}. The DINOv2 authors report that depth can be predicted well with the encoder
kept frozen and only a DPT decoder trained on top~\cite{dinov2}. Adapting a model trained on one task or domain to
another in this way is called transfer learning~\cite{yosinski}; training only the decoder, as we do, needs much less
memory and keeps the general visual features that the encoder learned from millions of images.

\subsection{Training on sensor depth}
Supervised depth models have long been trained on depth measured by a sensor rather than drawn by people: NYU
Depth~v2 provides indoor depth from a Microsoft Kinect camera~\cite{nyuv2}, KITTI provides street depth from a laser
scanner mounted on a car~\cite{kitti}, and Eigen et al.~\cite{eigen14} trained on both. What differs between such
studies is the quality of the sensor. On the iPhone~Pro and iPad~Pro, ARKit combines the LiDAR measurements with the
colour image into a depth map of $256\times192$ pixels with a confidence value for every pixel~\cite{arkitscenes}.
Studies that tested this LiDAR against established surveying methods found it accurate to about \SI{1}{cm} on objects
larger than \SI{10}{cm}~\cite{luetzenburg} and adequate for rapid architectural mapping at a scale of
1:200~\cite{spreafico}, which makes it a plausible teacher. ARKitScenes~\cite{arkitscenes} provides this depth together
with the depth of a Faro survey laser scanner~\cite{faro} for the same rooms, registered to the same camera frames, and
uses the Faro depth as ground truth for a task in which the LiDAR depth is upsampled with the help of the colour image.
Prompt Depth Anything~\cite{promptda}, the closest work to ours in data, likewise feeds iPhone LiDAR depth into a Depth
Anything model as an extra input. In both, the LiDAR must be present whenever the model runs; in this study it is used
only at training time, so the deployed model needs nothing but a colour image. Whether this noisier teacher helps a
large pretrained model as much as a laser scanner does, when both are judged against an independent laser reference,
is the subject of objectives~(1) and~(2).

\subsection{From depth maps to a room}
Fusing frames requires the camera pose of each frame, that is, its position and orientation. ARKit tracks the pose by
visual-inertial odometry (VIO), which follows image features from frame to frame and combines them with the device's
motion sensor~\cite{vinsmono}. Because the motion sensor measures acceleration in metric units, the tracked feature
points have positions in metres, and such sparse points can supply, frame by frame, the scale and offset that a
relative model lacks. Combining learned depth with the sparse geometry of a tracking system goes back to
CNN-SLAM~\cite{cnnslam}, and MonoSDF~\cite{monosdf} fits a scale and an offset to every image before using monocular
depth to guide surface reconstruction; the sparse-point alignment of Section~\ref{sec:design} follows this line.

The room itself is built by fusion into a truncated signed distance function (TSDF)~\cite{curless96}. Space is divided
into small cubes called voxels; each voxel stores its distance to the nearest observed surface, positive in front of
the surface and negative behind it, cut off (truncated) beyond a few voxels, and this value is averaged over every frame
that sees the voxel. The surface is then extracted where the average crosses zero, with the marching cubes
algorithm~\cite{marchingcubes}. KinectFusion~\cite{kinectfusion} made this fusion run in real time on Kinect depth,
and we use the implementation in Open3D~\cite{open3d}. Averaging suppresses noise that is random from frame to frame,
but frames that disagree in scale place the same wall at different distances, and the average then blurs or thickens
it; the consistency of scale across frames, which per-image metrics do not measure, therefore shapes the room. TSDF
fusion also does not depend on where the depth in metres comes from, which makes it a fair fixed stage for every depth
source.

Learned reconstruction systems replace parts of this pipeline with networks. Atlas~\cite{atlas} predicts the TSDF
directly from features of many posed images, NeuralRecon~\cite{neuralrecon} does so incrementally and in real time,
and SimpleRecon~\cite{simplerecon} estimates depth from several neighbouring frames before classic fusion; all are
evaluated on ScanNet~\cite{scannet}, a large collection of indoor colour-and-depth scans. Each needs several frames per
prediction and a network trained for its own pipeline, so they answer a different question from ours---what room a
single-image model yields when nothing else in the pipeline changes---and we do not compare against them.

\subsection{Measuring a reconstructed room}
A reconstructed room is compared with a reference surface through distances between points sampled on both. The
Chamfer distance~\cite{chamfer} averages, in both directions, the distance from each point to the nearest point of the
other surface. Tanks and Temples~\cite{tanks} instead fixes a distance threshold and reports precision, the share of
the reconstruction within the threshold of the reference; recall, the share of the reference within the threshold of
the reconstruction; and their harmonic mean, the F-score. A threshold ties the score to a use, which is how the
Introduction links 2, 5 and \SI{10}{cm} to renovation measurement, furniture placement and a room overview. Per frame,
we use AbsRel and \wone{} from the single-image literature~\cite{eigen14}.

\subsection{Position of this study}
In summary, prior work reports the per-image accuracy of depth models on standard benchmarks, adapts metric models
with labels from synthetic images or from the sensors of public datasets, feeds mobile LiDAR to a model at run time,
or tests mobile LiDAR itself against surveying methods. We did not find a study that combines these threads as this one
does: it uses the LiDAR of a consumer device only as a training teacher, compares it with a survey laser scanner as
teacher on the same rooms, and judges both against an independent laser reference, in centimetres on fused rooms as
well as per frame.
